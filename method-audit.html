Why the single score has no unit, and what replaces it

A reader asked what the atlas's "65" meant, in what unit, and how its inputs were weighted. He was right to ask: the score adds up quantities that share no unit, so it ranks moods, it does not measure anything. The fix is to show only three kinds of number, each with a unit the reader can check: the measured state of the world, the probability of named events over a fixed horizon, and the expected loss those events would cause.

1 · What the score is today

The tension score (57 on 30 Oct 2026) is the mean of severity × likelihood over the 55 active risks, each on a 1–5 scale, rescaled to 100. The earlier version (65) also added points for disaster alerts, earthquakes, space weather and media coverage.

Score = 100 / (25 · n) · Σi si · li    with si, li ∈ {1, …, 5}, n = 55  →  787 / (55 · 25) · 100 = 57.2

Five faults, in order of importance:

#FaultWhy it mattersWhere it shows in the atlas

What the score can still do: rank the atlas's own domains and show whether its author's assessments moved between two dates. What it cannot do: tell a reader what 57 means in the world.

2 · What a sound risk number is

In quantitative risk analysis a risk is a triplet (Kaplan & Garrick, 1981): what can happen, how likely it is, and what it would cost. The number that follows is the expected loss, and it has a unit because the consequence has one.

Ri = pi × Ci    pi ∈ [0, 1] over horizon H;   Ci in deaths, US$, people displaced…

Two properties make this usable, and both fail for the 1–5 score:

  • Expected losses add, whatever the dependence between events. Linearity of expectation holds even when the Iran war and the Hormuz closure are correlated, so a total expected loss is legitimate. Dependence changes the spread (the chance that several losses land in the same year), which needs a dependence model.
  • Probabilities are checkable. A forecast "80–95 % that Hormuz flows stay below half of pre-war levels through June 2027" resolves to true or false and can be scored. "Likelihood 5" cannot.

How published indices avoid the trap

The lesson is consistent: either measure in a common physical or monetary unit and add, or keep each probability attached to its own question and never add. A composite is acceptable only when its inputs are measured indicators and it says it is a composite, as INFORM does. Multiplying judgement ranks and averaging them across unrelated events is what the literature on risk matrices advises against: Thomas, Bratvold & Bickel (2014) found such matrices look "authoritative, and intellectually rigorous" without evidence that they perform.

3 · The proposal: three layers, each with a unit

Layer 2 — Forecasts of named events

Every risk is rewritten as a question that resolves to yes or no at a date, with a probability range and its source.

Ei: "X happens (or persists) before Ti"    pi ∈ [pilo, pihi]    source ∈ {agency, market, atlas}
  • A range, not a point, states the uncertainty honestly; the atlas's own estimates use IPCC-style bands (very unlikely < 10 %, unlikely 10–33 %, about as likely as not 33–66 %, likely 66–90 %, very likely > 90 %).
  • Each question has a resolution rule written down: which source decides, on which date.
  • Resolved questions are scored. The Brier score of a forecast p for an event that resolves to y ∈ {0, 1} is (p − y)²; the atlas publishes its running average, so readers can see whether its ≈ figures deserve trust.

Layer 3 — Expected impact, in units

Each event gets a consequence range per impact dimension, taken from scenario work by agencies (IMF, IEA, World Bank, WFP) where it exists, and labelled "atlas estimate" where it does not.

Ld = Σi pi · E[Ci,d | Ei]    d ∈ {deaths, US$ output lost, people in IPC3+, people displaced}
  • The total per dimension is a legitimate sum: expectations add regardless of dependence.
  • The spread and the tail need the dependence structure. The atlas already has it: the 100 cause-and-effect links become a Bayesian network (each link a conditional probability with a lag), and a Monte Carlo run over that network gives the distribution of each year's loss, the probability that at least one systemic shock lands in the next 12 months, and the 90th-percentile year.
P(at least one systemic shock in H) = 1 − Πi (1 − pi)  (independent)   or by Monte Carlo on the link network (dependent)

What is dropped. The 0–100 tension score, the 1–5 likelihood scale, and the sum of mixed live events. Severity survives only as a plain-language tag on each dossier (local / national / regional / global), never as a number that enters a formula.

What the headline shows. Three or four Layer-1 counts with their dates, the top Layer-2 probabilities with sources, and one Layer-3 line per dimension with its range. No single number.

4 · Worked example: the Iran → Hormuz → oil chain

Today the atlas scores this chain as four risks at 5 × 5 = 25 each. In the new units it is one root event with named consequences.

LayerTodayProposed
1 — measuredLikelihood 5, severity 5Hormuz flows 2.9 Mb/d on 11 Oct 2026, 83 % below the pre-war average; Brent 106.6 $/b on 14 Oct vs 70.9 $/b in February (atlas indicators, already sourced)
2 — forecast"Normal Hormuz flows restored by end-2026: ≈ 20 %" (atlas)Same question, as a range with a rule: "Monthly transits above 80 % of the 2025 average in December 2026, per Kpler/UKMTO: 10–25 % (atlas estimate)". Its complement, 75–90 % that the disruption persists, is the number a reader wants when asking "risk of what?"
3 — expected impactnoneFor the persisting case, one line per dimension with its source: world output lost (IMF or IEA scenario), extra people in IPC3+ via fertiliser and freight (WFP), and the household line the atlas already has for France. Expected impact = probability × consequence range.

The arithmetic, with an illustrative consequence (not a sourced figure): if an agency scenario puts the output loss of a persisting disruption at 0.5 % of world GDP, roughly US$ 0.55 trillion, the expected loss is 0.8 × 0.55 ≈ US$ 0.44 trillion over the horizon, with a range from the probability band (0.75–0.90) and the scenario band.

The cascade model keeps its role, with better clothes: each link becomes a conditional probability ("given a persisting Hormuz disruption, EU gas rationing in at least one member state this winter: 10–20 %") instead of the constant 0.82 × (0.6 + s/12.5). The network then drives the Monte Carlo run of Layer 3, and double counting disappears because oil, gas and fertiliser are consequences of one event, not four events.

7 · Limits that remain

  • Atlas estimates stay judgements. A probability band from the atlas is an informed guess until the track record shows otherwise. Phase 3 is what turns "≈ 15 %" into a claim with evidence behind it.
  • Consequence ranges are wide. Agency scenarios disagree, and many events (a Taiwan blockade) have no published loss estimate. The range must stay visible; a point estimate would smuggle the old problem back in.
  • Deaths and dollars are not one unit. The layers keep them apart on purpose. Any single "global risk number" would need a declared exchange rate between a life and a dollar, which is a value choice, not a measurement.
  • Conditional probabilities on 100 links are many parameters. Most will be atlas estimates at first. They should carry the same ≈ and band as everything else, and the Monte Carlo output should be labelled as depending on them.
  • Measured counts lag. UCDP conflict counts are yearly; IPC figures update by country on their own calendar. The date of each figure belongs next to it.

8 · In one paragraph

The first score multiplied and averaged 1–5 rankings across unrelated risks, then added earthquakes and media coverage with chosen weights: no unit, no meaning. The live events are already out, and the score itself goes next. What stays is three kinds of number, each with a unit: measured counts from the agencies that count them (conflicts, battle deaths, people in acute hunger), the probability of a named event by a date with its source and a resolution rule, and the expected loss in deaths, dollars or people, with its range. Nothing crosses from one kind to another, and every forecast is scored once it resolves, so readers can see whether the atlas's own estimates deserve trust.
